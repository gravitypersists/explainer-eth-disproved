The Exponential Time Hypothesis, and OpenAI's claim that it is false

For novices in complexity theory · about 12 minutes

A 27-year-old assumption about how hard one puzzle is has held up many results in theoretical computer science. OpenAI says one of its models has knocked it down. Here is what the assumption says, why it mattered, and how much weight the claim can bear today.

Chapter 1 / 9

A paper with a blunt title

On 26 October 2026, OpenAI made public a manuscript called The Exponential Time Hypothesis is False. Its author line says only “OpenAI”. According to OpenAI's own repository, it was produced by an unnamed, unreleased internal model, and a version of its main theorem has been checked by Lean, a program that verifies mathematical proofs step by step. It came out alongside two other claimed results, in mathematics and physics, which this piece leaves aside.

The unusual part is who is checking it. The hypothesis in the title was proposed in 1999 by Russell Impagliazzo and Ramamohan Paturi. The two of them, together with Ryan Williams, now lead an independent working group that will examine the proof. The materials were released immediately at the group's request, and the group has reportedly said that taking part is not an endorsement. Until that review is done, this is a claim, and the rest of this page treats it as one.

To make sense of it, we'll take four questions in order:

  1. What is the puzzle at the centre of all this?
  2. What exactly is the Exponential Time Hypothesis, or ETH?
  3. Why did anyone care whether it was true?
  4. What would follow if OpenAI is right, and how sure can we be?
Chapter 2 / 9

The puzzle: three switches, four rules

Start with something you can solve in your head. There are three switches, x, y and z, and each one is either T (true) or F (false). There are four rules. A rule is satisfied when at least one of its parts holds. Your goal is a setting of the switches that satisfies all four rules at once.

How to read NOT and OR

“x OR y OR z” is satisfied when at least one of x, y, z is T. “NOT x” holds when x is F. So “NOT x OR y” is satisfied unless x is T and y is F at the same time.

Predict

There are 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 ways to set the three switches. How many of them light all four rules?

Exactly 2: (x, y, z) = (F, F, T) and (F, T, T). The only way to be sure was to check every setting, which is what the sweep below does. That's brute force. With 3 switches it's 8 rows; every extra switch doubles the table.

What you just solved is an instance of 3-SAT. A 3-SAT formula is a list of rules joined by AND, where each rule (computer scientists say clause) is an OR of at most three variables or their negations. The question is whether some true/false setting, called a satisfying assignment, makes every clause true.

The sure-fire method is the one the table used: try every setting. With n variables there are 2n2^n2n settings, so brute force takes on the order of 2n2^n2n steps. Three switches give 8 rows; thirty give about a billion. This matters beyond toy switches: the paper itself calls k-SAT “the canonical NP-complete problem”, the standard example of the hard problems behind the P vs NP question.

Chapter 4 / 9

The bet: “you can't get under the wall”

Clever algorithms have lowered the base of the exponent, but none has escaped the exponential shape. In 1999, Russell Impagliazzo and Ramamohan Paturi turned that into a precise bet. The Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH) says that no algorithm solves 3-SAT in subexponential time: some exponential cost, cnc^ncn for some ccc greater than 1, can't be avoided. (A running time is subexponential if it grows more slowly than cnc^ncn for every c>1c > 1c>1. An example is 2n2^{\sqrt{n}}2n​.)

Notice the word hypothesis. Nobody has proved ETH. It is an unproven assumption, a foundation stone that researchers chose to build on.

You have probably heard of a famous cousin, P ≠ NP. Both are claims about how hard 3-SAT is, but they rule out different things. The ladder sorts running times from fast at the bottom to slow at the top.

P ≠ NP says 3-SAT has no polynomial-time algorithm. Nothing on the bottom rung.

ETH says much more: nothing anywhere below the exponential rungs. Even 2n2^{\sqrt{n}}2n​, which eventually outgrows any polynomial, is ruled out.

So ETH is the stronger bet. If ETH is true, P ≠ NP follows. The reverse doesn't hold.

The formal statement

ETH: there is some constant s>0s > 0s>0 such that every algorithm that correctly solves 3-SAT needs at least 2s⋅n2^{s \cdot n}2s⋅n steps on some formulas with n variables. Equivalently, there is no 2o(n)2^{o(n)}2o(n) algorithm for 3-SAT, where o(n)o(n)o(n) means something that grows more slowly than any fixed fraction of n.

Apply

Suppose someone proved that 3-SAT can be solved in about 2n2^{\sqrt{n}}2n​ steps. Which assumption would be broken?

ETH only. 2n2^{\sqrt{n}}2n​ grows more slowly than any cnc^ncn, so it lands in ETH's forbidden zone. But it still grows faster than any polynomial, so it doesn't reach the P = NP rung. That's why a broken ETH, by itself, says nothing about P vs NP. It's the trap headlines fall into.

Chapter 5 / 9

What was built on the bet

Researchers usually can't prove outright that a problem has no fast algorithm. (If they could do that for 3-SAT, P vs NP would be settled.) What they can prove is a link: if this problem had a much faster algorithm, so would 3-SAT.

Take graph colouring: can you colour the dots of a network with a few colours so that no two linked dots share one? A typical result reads:

Graph colouring has no 2o(n)2^{o(n)}2o(n) algorithm on graphs with n vertices, unless ETH fails.

The proof is a translation. It turns any 3-SAT formula into a colouring problem of comparable size, so a much faster colouring algorithm would also be a much faster 3-SAT algorithm, and that would break ETH. As long as ETH holds, colouring is stuck with exponential time too. The same move works for many classic problems; the diagram shows a few of them.

Some results lean on a stronger bet. The Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis (SETH) says that as the clauses get wider (4-SAT, 5-SAT and so on), the best algorithms get ever closer to brute force's 2n2^n2n. A famous result rests on it: Backurs and Indyk proved that edit distance, the measure of how different two strings are a close relative of what diff computes, can't be computed in much less than quadratic time, unless SETH fails.

Why is SETH stronger?

ETH only says 3-SAT needs some exponential cnc^ncn with c>1c > 1c>1, and ccc could be close to 1. SETH says that for wide enough clauses the base gets arbitrarily close to 2. If SETH is true, wide-clause SAT needs exponential time, and that in turn forces 3-SAT to need exponential time, which is ETH. So SETH implies ETH.

Explain · think, then reveal

A paper proves “graph colouring has no 2o(n)2^{o(n)}2o(n) algorithm, unless ETH fails.” In your own words: what did it actually prove, and what would make the statement stop telling us anything about hardness?

It proved a link, not hardness itself: any much-faster colouring algorithm could be turned into a much-faster 3-SAT algorithm. Colouring is “hard” only as long as ETH is true. If ETH fails, the statement is still true, but it no longer shows that colouring is hard.

The claim

Now OpenAI's claim, stated precisely. Three symbols first. n is the number of variables and m the number of clauses. log⁡n\log nlogn is the logarithm of n, which grows very slowly: going from a thousand variables to a million only doubles it. And o(1)o(1)o(1) is a small term that shrinks toward zero as n grows.

The manuscript claims a deterministic algorithm (no coin flips) that counts the satisfying assignments of any SAT formula whose clauses have a fixed width k, 3-SAT included, using only a polynomial amount of memory. Its running time is

(n+m)O(1)⋅2 n/(log⁡n)1/2+o(1)(n+m)^{O(1)} \cdot 2^{\,n / (\log n)^{1/2 + o(1)}}(n+m)O(1)⋅2n/(logn)1/2+o(1)

The first factor is an ordinary polynomial, which barely matters next to the second. In plain terms the cost is roughly 2n/log⁡n2^{n/\sqrt{\log n}}2n/logn​.

Counting is at least as hard as deciding: if the count is above zero, the formula is satisfiable. And n/log⁡nn/\sqrt{\log n}n/logn​ grows more slowly than any fixed fraction of n, so the 3-SAT case is a 2o(n)2^{o(n)}2o(n) algorithm for 3-SAT. That is exactly what ETH forbids. The repository's summary puts it in one line: “The case of 3-SAT refutes the Exponential Time Hypothesis.”

What the paper actually proves

The theorem covers every fixed clause width k≥2k \ge 2k≥2: a deterministic algorithm counts the satisfying assignments of a k-CNF formula (a k-SAT formula) with n variables and m clauses in the time above, in polynomial space. The main theorem gives 2Ok(nlog⁡log⁡n/log⁡n)2^{O_k(n \log\log n / \sqrt{\log n})}2Ok​(nloglogn/logn​) in general, improving to 2O(n/log⁡n)2^{O(n/\sqrt{\log n})}2O(n/logn​) when the number of clauses is at most a constant times n. The paper states that this refutes ETH, SETH, and two related hypotheses, Gap-ETH and NSETH.

Predict

The claimed algorithm is subexponential, and the old best was exponential. When do you expect it to beat the old best (1.307n1.307^n1.307n) on real formulas?

Only at enormous sizes. Even with every hidden constant generously set to 1, the new bound only edges past 1.307n1.307^n1.307n at around 800 variables for sparse formulas, and around n≈1079n \approx 10^{79}n≈1079 for the general bound. By then both step counts are astronomically large, and nobody knows the real constants, which could push the crossover much further out. The exponent shrinks by only about a log⁡n\sqrt{\log n}logn​ factor. Real SAT solvers also don't run worst-case algorithms; they exploit structure in real instances. The win is about the shape of the curve, not the speed. (Press coverage, e.g. Unite.AI, says turning this into practical speedups would take substantial further research.)

Chapter 7 / 9

If it holds: what falls, what stands

Back to the foundation. Suppose review confirms the claim. What happens to everything that rested on ETH?

Predict

The famous result that edit distance (a close relative of what diff computes) can't be done much faster than quadratic time assumes SETH, not ETH. If OpenAI's claim holds, what happens to that result?

It loses its basis. SETH is the stronger assumption, and SETH implies ETH, so if ETH is false, SETH is false too. The paper says it refutes SETH directly. Yet the quadratic bound itself still stands. Backurs and Indyk's proof actually starts from the Orthogonal Vectors Conjecture (OVC), a weaker assumption that this claim doesn't touch, so the bound keeps a footing on OVC. The same holds for longest common subsequence and regex matching. And losing a basis would never have meant “edit distance is now fast”: no faster algorithm comes with the claim.

The diagram updates once you've answered.

The theorem blocks keep their statements; what they lose is their footing. Each link of the form “a faster algorithm here would break ETH” still holds, but with ETH gone it no longer shows that the problem is hard. A few bounds, edit distance among them, keep a footing because they also follow from OVC.

Three tempting conclusions

“So P = NP?”

No. The claimed running time, roughly 2n/log⁡n2^{n/\sqrt{\log n}}2n/logn​, is still far above any polynomial, and ETH failing says nothing either way about P vs NP. The paper itself notes that its result doesn't imply subexponential algorithms for general NP problems, because the usual translations between problems can blow up the input size.

“So crypto is broken?”

No. RSA and AES never rested on ETH. And “subexponential” can still mean astronomically slow: factoring, the problem behind RSA, has had a subexponential algorithm, the general number field sieve (GNFS), for decades, and properly sized RSA keys are still secure.

“So Hamiltonian cycle and the rest are fast now?”

No. Losing the proof that a problem is hard is not the same as finding a fast algorithm for it. A bound whose condition fails is unproven, not reversed. Any faster algorithm for a particular problem still has to be found and proved on its own.

Is it true? What “verified” does and doesn't mean

“Verified in Lean” is strong evidence. It is also narrower than it sounds. Lean is a proof assistant: you state a theorem in its formal language, supply a proof, and its small trusted core, the kernel, checks every step. OpenAI's repository includes the formal statement as a challenge file and invites anyone to re-run the check with a tool called Comparator. Here is what that check does and doesn't settle.

Explain · think, then reveal

Lean's checker accepted the proof. Name one way the result could still turn out to be wrong, or weaker than advertised.

Lean only confirms that the formal theorem follows. Humans still have to confirm that the formal definitions (the machine model, how a formula is encoded, what “time” counts) mean what the paper says. A faithful-looking but subtly off definition could make the checked theorem say less than the headline. (Separately, the checked bound is already slightly weaker than the paper's headline bound, though still enough to refute ETH.)

ETH's own inventors are among those reviewing it, and they say their involvement isn't a certification.

What to watch for

  • The working group's write-up of the proof.
  • Whether experts judge the Lean definitions, the machine model and the formula encoding, to be faithful.
  • Independent re-checks and re-implementations of the argument.
  • Whether the paper's sharper bound, the one that wasn't formalised, holds up.

One more piece of context, about the source rather than this result: on 7 October 2026 the same repository withdrew three unrelated manuscripts after a sign error was found. That says nothing about the ETH proof. It is a reminder that these manuscripts are, in the README's words, “best understood as a form of raw research data”.

Recap

  • 3-SAT asks whether some setting satisfies every clause. Brute force doubles its work with each variable.
  • ETH bet that no algorithm gets 3-SAT under an exponential wall. It was always an assumption, never a theorem.
  • Many hardness results were built on that bet, and SETH, a stronger bet, held up others such as edit distance.
  • OpenAI claims an algorithm just under the wall. If review confirms it, the ETH-based results become unproven again and SETH falls too, though some bounds, edit distance's among them, still stand on a weaker assumption (OVC). P vs NP and today's cryptography are untouched.

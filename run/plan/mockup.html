The Exponential Time Hypothesis, and OpenAI's claim that it is false

For Hacker News readers (novice in complexity theory) · plan v2 · estimated ~15.5 min (built v1 ~13.4; brief 12; cuts deferred to the user per d-07) · scrollytelling in 9 chapters, re-skinned ToC rail on desktop

Shape: A story told in order: a puzzle → the wall it hits → the bet that the wall is permanent (ETH) → what was built on the bet → the claim that breaks it → the fallout → how far to trust it. Sticky canvases advance as you scroll (presentational, as requested). Predict-then-reveal cards sit at the key ideas and at each misconception, so the reader doesn't just watch. Rejected: a full-page explorable or SAT-solver sandbox. It's slower to learn, the user asked for presentation, and the new algorithm's speedup can't be shown at any size a browser can run.

Framing (d-02): it is always "OpenAI's claim", never "the proof that ETH is false". Press-only details (dates, "practical speedups need further research") are attributed as reported. No count of dependent papers is given.

Objectives:

  • o-1 Explain 3-SAT with a concrete example, and why brute force takes ~2^n. checks: k-01, k-02
  • o-2 State ETH in plain words and tell it apart from P≠NP. checks: k-03, k-08
  • o-3 Explain why ETH matters (conditional lower bounds, and the bolder SETH) with an example or two. checks: k-04, k-09, k-06, k-08
  • o-4 Explain what OpenAI claims, its verification status, and what would and wouldn't follow. checks: k-05, k-06, k-07, k-08

s-02

The puzzle: three switches, four rules

concrete → predict → name → familyo-1o-3changed in v2

Solve the puzzle by hand, name it in plain words before the label, then show it stands for a whole family.

"Here are three switches and four rules. Every rule must be on. Try it." (Switchboard and k-01, as built.)

Naming (new): "What you just solved is a satisfiability puzzle: yes/no switches, and rules that each say at least one of these must hold. Can some setting satisfy every rule at once? When each rule mentions at most three switches, computer scientists call it 3-SAT: SAT for satisfiability, 3 for three per rule. The sure-fire method is the table: with n switches, 2ⁿ settings to try."

Subhead (new): One puzzle stands for a whole family. "Checking a setting took a glance. Finding one meant trying settings. Many famous problems share that shape: quick to check, apparently a search to find. Colour a network so no two linked dots match. Plan a route through every city exactly once. And the hardest of them can be rewritten as one another, at about the same size. That's why 3-SAT matters far beyond switches: its own paper calls it 'the canonical NP-complete problem'. A fast way to solve it would hand you fast ways to solve the whole family."

Disclosure (new style): ▸ More · How to read NOT and OR show

PREDICT · k-01
There are 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 ways to set the three switches. How many of them light all four rules? Options: None of them / Exactly 2 / About half (4) / All 8.
Feedback: Exactly 2: (F,F,T) and (F,T,T). The only way to be sure was to check every setting: brute force. New line: Notice the lopsidedness: checking one setting is a glance, but finding a good one meant trying them. Every extra switch doubles the table.

The doubling wall

scrolly worked example · B1o-1changed in v2

Make 2ⁿ look as daunting as it is, on a linear scale. Show the best known algorithm only shortens the number.

The step cards now carry the time text (H-01):

1 · n=3: "8 rows. Instant: you just checked them by hand."

2 · n=20: "About a million rows: a laptop does it in well under a second. And look what happened to your 8 rows."

3 · n=100: "The bar for 20 switches has vanished. A billion computers, each checking a billion rows a second: tens of thousands of years."

[k-02 gate] 4 · n=110: "Ten more switches, 1,024× the work: tens of millions of years."

5 · n=1000: "No bar can hold this, so here is the number written out: 302 digits. The atoms in the observable universe number about 10⁸⁰, an 81-digit number."

6 · "The best known algorithm before OpenAI's claim (PPSZ family) takes about 1.307ⁿ steps: at n=1000, a 117-digit number. A smaller multiplier per switch, ×1.307 instead of ×2, but still a fixed one. A lower wall, not a way around it."

PREDICT · k-02 · unchanged
Brute force on 100 switches already needs about 10^30 checks. Add 10 more switches. How much more work is that? Options: About 10% more (tempting) / About 10× more / About 1,000× more.
Feedback: 2^10 = 1,024. 110 is only 10% bigger than 100, but each switch doubles the table, ten times over.

s-04

The bet: "you can't get under the wall"

question → ranked candidates · B1 → applyo-2changed in v2

Ask where the best possible 3-SAT algorithm could sit; show ETH and P≠NP as bets about that, with ETH the bolder one.

"Clever algorithms have lowered the multiplier per switch, from ×2 to about ×1.307, but every one found so far still pays some fixed multiplier for each extra switch. Is that a law, or have we just not been clever enough? Nobody knows the fastest possible algorithm for 3-SAT. Here are four candidate answers, sorted by how quickly the work grows as you add switches. Two are algorithms we have. Two are speeds nobody has achieved."

"In 1999, Russell Impagliazzo and Ramamohan Paturi made a precise bet, the Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH): every algorithm for 3-SAT, however clever, pays at least some fixed multiplier for each extra switch. It might be tiny, ×1.001, but it never fades away. Nobody has proved it. It's an assumption."

Steps: (1) four candidates; (2) P ≠ NP's bet: "somewhere above n³"; (3) ETH's bet: "above ETH's line", a shorter bracket, so the bolder bet.

Disclosure: ▸ More · The formal statement show

APPLY · k-03
Suppose someone proved that 3-SAT can be solved in about 2^√n steps. Which assumption would be broken? Options: P ≠ NP (so P = NP) (tempting) / ETH only / Both / Neither.
Feedback: With 2^√n the multiplier per switch fades toward ×1, so it sits below ETH's line, where ETH bet nothing could be. But it still grows faster than any polynomial, above n³, so P ≠ NP is untouched. A broken ETH alone says nothing about P vs NP.

s-05

What was built on the bet

worked conditional → explain → SETH → compareo-3changed in v2

Show ETH is load-bearing using a family member the reader met; then give SETH a proper, deliberate introduction, because chapter 7 depends on it.

Subhead: A link, not a proof. "Researchers usually can't prove outright that a problem has no fast algorithm. What they can prove is a link. Remember chapter 2's family, and graph colouring in it? Because colouring and 3-SAT can be rewritten as one another at about the same size, a result reads: Graph colouring has no 2^{o(n)} algorithm on n-vertex graphs, unless ETH fails. A much faster colouring algorithm would be a much faster 3-SAT algorithm, and that would break ETH."

EXPLAIN · k-04 · think, then reveal · unchanged
A paper proves "graph colouring has no 2^{o(n)} algorithm, unless ETH fails." In your own words: what did it actually prove, and what would make the statement stop telling us anything about hardness?
Model answer: It proved a link: a much faster colouring algorithm would give a much faster 3-SAT algorithm. Colouring is "hard" only while ETH holds. If ETH fails, the statement is still true but no longer shows hardness.

Subhead (new): A bolder bet: SETH. "ETH only says 3-SAT's multiplier never fades to ×1. There's a much bolder bet, the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis (SETH): let the rules get wider, 4, 5, 10 switches each, and the best possible multiplier climbs all the way to ×2. For wide rules, brute force is essentially unbeatable. Bolder bets buy stronger results. The most famous: Backurs and Indyk proved that edit distance (a close relative of what diff computes) can't be done much faster than quadratic time, unless SETH fails. Hold on to SETH and edit distance; they come back in chapter 7."

Disclosure: ▸ More · Why does SETH imply ETH? show

COMPARE · k-09 (new)
ETH and SETH are both unproven bets. Which one is riskier, that is, easier to turn out wrong? Options: ETH: it's the original / SETH: it claims more / Equally risky: they're about the same problem.
Feedback: SETH. It claims more: not just "some fixed multiplier per switch" but "×2 for wide rules". A bigger claim has more ways to fail, and anything that breaks ETH breaks SETH too, since SETH implies ETH. The payoff is stronger results, like the edit-distance bound. Keep this in mind for chapter 7.

s-06

The claim

statement → ladder → predict → charto-4changed in v2

Say exactly what's claimed and where it lands: below ETH's line, nowhere near polynomial.

"OpenAI's claim: a deterministic algorithm that counts the solutions of any 3-SAT formula, or any fixed-width k-SAT, in roughly

(n+m)^{O(1)} · 2^( n / (log n)^{1/2 + o(1)} )

steps, using only polynomial memory. If you can count solutions you can tell whether there is one. In per-switch terms, its multiplier fades toward ×1 as n grows: very slowly, but it fades. That is exactly what ETH bet could never happen."

PREDICT · k-06
The famous result that edit distance (a close relative of what diff computes) can't be done much faster than quadratic time assumes SETH, not ETH. s-08

Is it true? What "verified" does and doesn't mean

contrast · B4 → explaino-4

Teach the reader to read "verified in Lean" correctly, and what to watch for.

"'Verified in Lean' is strong evidence, and it's also narrower than it sounds."

Moved here in v2 (H-09): a compact "How the claim surfaced" row above the review card: 16 Oct, model produces the claimed disproof (as reported) · 17 Oct, formalised in Lean (as reported) · 25 Oct, date on the manuscript · 26 Oct, made public and review group announced.

After the table: the review group's own words, as reported: "Our involvement should not be taken as certification of the claim." Base rate: the same repo withdrew three unrelated manuscripts on 7 Oct after a sign error.

What to watch for: the working group's write-up · whether the Lean definitions are judged faithful · independent re-checks · whether the paper's sharper bound holds.

Collapsed: "The exact formal bound" (Lean proves 2^{A·n(log log n)²/√log n}, slightly weaker than the headline but still enough to refute ETH).

EXPLAIN · k-07 · think, then reveal
Lean's checker accepted the proof. Name one way the result could still turn out to be wrong, or weaker than advertised.
Model answer: Lean confirms that the formal theorem follows. Humans must confirm that the formal definitions (machine model, formula encoding, what "time" counts) mean what the paper says. A subtly off definition could make the checked theorem say less than the headline. ETH's own inventors are among those reviewing it, and they say their involvement isn't a certification.

s-09

The whole story in five statements

interleaved retrieval → recapo-2o-3o-4

Retrieve across all objectives, then close.

SORT · k-08 · each statement committed separately
Suppose the claim survives review. For each statement, does it follow?
(a) P = NP → doesn't follow (far above polynomial)
(b) 3-SAT can be solved in 2^{o(n)} time → follows (that's the claim)
(c) RSA encryption is broken → doesn't follow (never rested on ETH; subexponential ≠ fast)
(d) The hardness results proved "unless ETH fails" become unproven, not false → follows
(e) SETH is false too → follows (SETH implies ETH)
Each answer's feedback points back to its chapter.

Recap (4 lines): 3-SAT is the switches puzzle, and brute force doubles per switch. ETH bet that every 3-SAT algorithm pays a fixed multiplier per extra switch; SETH bet that for wide rules it's ×2. Many hardness results were built on those bets. OpenAI claims an algorithm just under the wall: if review confirms it, those results become unproven again, SETH falls too (some bounds, edit distance among them, still stand on OVC), and P vs NP and your crypto are untouched.

Further reading: OpenAI's math repo (manuscript, Lean files) · Wikipedia, "Exponential time hypothesis".
